My first Level 2 attempt failed right in front of me: the drogue came out, the main never did. I found the cause, restaged it, and flew again. The second flight went to 4,135 feet and earned my Level 2.

Objective

Gain experience designing, manufacturing, and constructing a rocket capable of withstanding a launch on a motor between 640 and 5,120 newton-seconds. Obtain the Level 2 High Power Rocketry certification. Obtain and analyze flight data to improve the design and construction of the next vehicle.

Challenges faced

Setbacks were plenty on this one. The motor was four times the power of the Level 1 certification I had earned that spring, the electronics were completely new to me, and I wanted to step up from a single parachute to a dual-deploy system, with the drogue coming out at apogee and the main at 1,500 feet.

Sizing the fins

I cut a first set of fins, then went back to the simulation and found the stability margin sitting lower than I wanted, which meant the fins were simply too small for the airframe. I kept the root chord at 4.30 inches and raised the span from 3.10 to 3.50 inches, taking the fin area from 8.72 to 10.00 square inches. That is a 15 percent increase in area, and it brought the static margin up near the 2 calibers I was aiming for.

L2 — first attempt at certification

Over time, I designed, built, and flew the first iteration of the L2-capable rocket. The result? The main chute never opened. An incorrect separation during recovery prevented it from deploying at its programmed altitude. Luckily, the drogue chute worked — enough to save the airframe, but not the fins. I was frustrated. Half my summer was sitting around me in pieces.

The rocket flew on 18 July 2026 at the Lucerne Valley lakebed with the Rocketry Organization of California, using the same AeroTech J250W I would later fly for the Level 2 certification, and it reached 4,224 feet. The flight record says the rest of it plainly. The main charge fired where it was supposed to, at 102.5 seconds and 694 feet, and the continuity voltage on that channel drops from 8.68 volts to 0.02 volts inside a single 20 millisecond sample, which is the e-match burning through. What did not change was the descent rate. The rocket was coming down at 40.5 feet per second under the drogue before the charge fired, and it was still coming down at 40.5 feet per second afterward, meaning the canopy never inflated and the vehicle rode the drogue the whole way to the ground.

It landed hard enough to break the fins. One came off the airframe entirely, a second was damaged, a third cracked at the root, and the fourth was untouched.

Finding the cause

I took the rocket home and spent weeks going through the data and the wreckage before I was willing to say what had happened. The separation point for the main parachute was in the wrong place. The first time around I had accounted for the length of the shock cord but not for the shroud lines attached to the parachute. I believed that the shock cord alone would be enough to pull the main chute out of the airframe, but the shroud lines got pulled first, and their length acted to disperse the pull on the parachute, so the canopy never cleared the upper airframe, which was still attached to the nosecone.

Nothing needed to be stronger. I restaged the recovery train so that the main parachute sits directly behind the nosecone, and at separation the nosecone pops off and frees the canopy directly.

L2 — second attempt and certification flight

The second attempt is the one that earned my Level 2: a J250 motor to 4,135 feet, main deployment at 1,498 feet, and a descent rate of 19 ft/s, right on target. As an additional testament to the design and construction, the rocket stayed within 6.8 degrees of vertical at burnout, meaning that it flew straight up until burnout. The recovery system did exactly what I designed it to do. The main parachute deployed at 1,498 feet, the altitude I had programmed it to, and brought the rocket down at 19 feet per second.

The electronics bay I designed and 3D-printed protected the altimeter and GPS through launch, both ejection charges, and a 59.6 G touchdown, and the GPS tracker maintained tracking through recovery.

How close the prediction was

I had simulated the flight in OpenRocket before I flew it, and afterward I could check the prediction against the record. The model said 4,561 feet and 705 feet per second, and the rocket flew 4,135 feet and 658 feet per second, so I was 9 percent high on altitude and 7 percent high on speed. The landing velocity it predicted was 19.9 feet per second against the 19 I measured. I had not thought of it as a prediction-then-check exercise at the time, but that is what it was, and it is the closest I have come so far to knowing how a vehicle would behave before building it.

What I learned

The failure on the first attempt taught me to be more thorough in my designing but more specifically my testing. This kind of mistake could have been avoided through more rigorous testing and by verifying that the parachute gets pulled out during separation testing on the ground. All in all, what has stayed with me is this: failure is a lesson waiting to be discovered. I prepared what I could, but it was watching the failure of my rocket that taught me the final lesson.

My second rocket reached Mach 1.2 and broke apart in flight. I recovered the electronics bay intact, pulled the full 500 Hz flight record off it, and spent weeks working out from the data and the wreckage that the fins failed first and the airframe opened afterward.

Objective

Design and build a rocket from scratch capable of passing Mach 1, fly it supersonic, and recover it with a complete flight record on board. Prove the airframe, the recovery system, and the electronics on a subsonic flight first, before risking the vehicle on the supersonic attempt. Add two successful flights to the NAR Level 2 HPR Flight Log.

The vehicle

I built this one straight after earning my Level 2 certification. I designed the vehicle from scratch around a 3.1 inch Blue Tube airframe and a 54 mm fiberglass motor mount, with a dual-deploy recovery system and a custom 3D-printed electronics bay holding the Blue Raven altimeter and the Eggfinder GPS tracker. I checked the design in OpenRocket, which put the static margin at 3.37 calibers and predicted 1,497 ft/s on an AeroTech L1000W, ~2,714 N·s, sized the ejection charges for the airframe volume, and sourced custom G10 fiberglass centering rings for the airframe.

The staged approach

Rather than flying the rocket straight to its design limit, I flew it on a smaller motor first as a subsonic shakedown. That flight reached 2,833 feet, separated at apogee, and deployed the main parachute at 1,499 feet for an 18 ft/s descent, which confirmed that the airframe, the recovery system, and the electronics all worked before I risked the vehicle on a supersonic attempt.

Before either flight I ground-tested the ejection charges in the actual airframe, on 22 July, with the section resting on a stand and cushions set downrange to catch it. Four tests, all successful.

What happened

One week later I flew the supersonic attempt. The rocket reached 1,367 feet per second, about Mach 1.2, at 2.14 seconds into the flight, and broke apart at about 2.20 seconds — still under thrust, 81 percent of the way through a 2.72 second burn, near maximum dynamic pressure. The avionics bay came down intact, and I was able to pull the complete flight record off it: 500 Hz inertial data and 50 Hz barometric data covering the whole flight.

What the data showed

I spent weeks going through that data and the wreckage. The data showed that side loading went from 3 G to 88 G in about a third of a second, the roll rate decayed from about 535 to 290 deg/s, and all four fins were missing from what I recovered.

My first read was that the airframe wasn't strong enough — but the roll rate started dropping before the airframe came apart, which meant the fins failed first and the airframe opened afterward. I had to throw out my first explanation and go with what the data showed.

The altitude that wasn't real

The altimeter's summary file reported a peak altitude of 7,131 feet, far above what actually happened. When the airframe opened, the static port was exposed, and the pressure reading started swinging between 0.72 and 0.86 atm from one 20 ms sample to the next, which is not an altitude change. By cross-checking it against the backup inertial data I was able to establish the real trajectory, and I threw the corrupted value out. The barometric channel was not trustworthy before the breakup either: at Mach 1.2 the pressure at a flush static port is not free-stream static, and that channel reads roughly a thousand feet high. Integrating the inertial record instead puts the breakup at about 1,800 feet.

What I changed

I specified the redesign from what the data showed: thicker, stiffer fins with a stronger root attachment, and a composite airframe replacing the original tube. The changes I ended up making were materials and manufacturing decisions rather than changes to the shape of the rocket. The rebuild is in progress and has not flown yet.

What I learned

What has stayed with me from this flight is that the data has the final say. My first explanation was wrong, the number the altimeter handed me was wrong, and the only way to get to the real answer was to go through the record channel by channel and let it correct me. Typically, I design a part, build it, fly it, and use the data from that flight to decide what needs to change for the next version. This flight is the clearest example I have of that loop.

The program

Bronco Launch is Cal Poly Pomona's high power rocketry organization, and it was called UMBRA until this year. Bronco Avionics is the team inside it responsible for the flight electronics, and Launch Operations is the sub-team I co-lead with Gregorio Marin. The goal of Project EXO Bronco is to fly a two-stage rocket to the Kármán line at 100 km. The vehicle we have in hand is MTD3, which flew once in February 2026 and is being prepared to fly again. The next vehicle, EXO 0, is in preliminary design.

Four of us, me included, are responsible for the Blue Raven altimeters that fire the deployment charges on both stages and light the sustainer, the Featherweight GPS, the Sprinklemini telemetry boards, the ground stations, and the launch-day command sequence. The avionics bay itself is ours too, which means the harness, the terminals, the switches, and the batteries. I joined the sub-team in March 2026 and became co-lead in May.

The pop test

On 30 August 2026 we ran a black powder deployment test on MTD3, preparing it to fly again, and both stages fired correctly. I worked from the Pop Test Procedure I wrote with Ria Malik, integrated the sleds onto the rocket after Greg re-harnessed them, and connected to the Blue Ravens to command the test.

That test is the one piece of this vehicle's electronics I can tell you has been exercised on real hardware rather than on a bench, and the one change of mine it exercised was the screw terminals.

The terminals that had corroded

The booster's main deployment channel had lost continuity. I traced it to the barrier screw terminal strips in the bay. They were not the wrong part for the job. They were simply corroded with age, so I replaced them like for like with new 12-position strips. That is a replacement and not a redesign, and I would rather call it what it is. It held through both charges on 30 August.

Three other changes are in the new bay and none of them have been tested yet: new batteries, a voltage regulator, and a bay layout that changed to fit the batteries. The layout came out of my meetings with the structures team, and structures holds the CAD for it.

The board that did not need erasing

The sub-team's procedure for reprogramming a Sprinklemini opened by erasing the board. In September I put one on the bench and checked what state it was actually in before doing that. It was already running CircuitPython 9.1.3, it had no boot.py on it, and it was neither locked nor broken, so erasing it would have wiped a working configuration to fix nothing. The replacement procedure I wrote runs about twenty-five illustrated steps and supersedes the older versions.

Checking that board is also how I found the flight code sitting in exactly one place. It had been on the board since 7 May with no copy anywhere else, which meant losing the board meant losing the program's flight software. I backed it up on 15 September.

What is still open

Two anomalies from the February flight are the reason MTD3 has not flown again. The two Sprinklemini boards cannot both be received at once, and the code shows why, because both stages downlink on 434.0 MHz. I documented that and reproduced it on the bench, where powering one board off made the other's data appear. The fix is identified and not yet implemented. The second is a Blue Raven that did not transmit its high rate record on one stage, and that is waiting on a re-test.

Neither of those is my failure. They were here before I was. They are what I inherited along with the hardware, and getting them closed is the job.

What I have written

I set up the Drive structure the sub-team files into and the naming convention it runs on, and I wrote the avionics bay design constraints for EXO 0. The pop test procedure is mine and Ria Malik's, and the Sprinklemini reprogramming procedure is mine. The flight code on the Sprinkleminis is Tim's and Aivie's. I maintain it, document it, and work on it alongside Greg.

Objective

Fly as close as possible to a 2,800 foot target altitude on the parts the competition supplied, and record altitude on board through the whole flight.

The competition

The Small Rocket Competition was my first rocket. UMBRA gave each team a target altitude of 2,800 feet and a common parts list, and the team that came closest won. We were meant to be five people, but one dropped before the design phase, so we finished as four.

What I did, and what I did not

I led the team. That meant running the schedule, deciding what had to be finished before each meeting, assigning the research and design work, and reviewing the SolidWorks files and giving the final go-ahead before anything went to the printer. Eric did the SolidWorks modeling. I did not know SolidWorks well enough at that point to do it myself, so my part was reviewing and approving it rather than drawing it. Once we reached construction, the four of us built the rocket together.

The two things that were mine end to end were the avionics bay and the code. I laid out the bay on a printed sled, harnessed it, held the boards down with rubber bands, and used quarters as ballast to balance the weight of the 9 V battery. The competition supplied the parts — an Adafruit Metro Mini 328 V2, a BMP390 pressure sensor, and a micro SD breakout — and I wrote the flight code that ran on them: about eighty lines of Arduino C that read barometric altitude and wrote a time and altitude pair to the SD card every 0.25 seconds, in a format that opened directly in Excel.

The altimeter that would not work

My altimeter worked briefly on the day it was handed to us and then stopped. I took it home to fix it and could not get it to read at all. I worked through the wiring against the pinout, moved it from SPI to I2C, ran a bus scan that showed SDA and SCL both sitting high with no device answering, and stripped it back to the sensor alone with the SD breakout removed. The code compiled and ran, and the sensor was simply not there on the bus. I suspected the hardware rather than the code, but suspecting is not proving. A teammate suggested the test that settled it: run my code on another team's altimeter. It worked the first time. That isolated the fault to my hardware, UMBRA replaced the unit, and the replacement flew.

What happened on the flight

The rocket flew on 23 November 2025 and reached 2,514 feet against the 2,800 foot target, measured by a separate altimeter the competition supplied. The logger I built recorded the whole ascent. On score we were first among the disqualified teams and would have placed second overall.

We were disqualified because the motor came out of the rocket. The boat tail was printed in PLA and doubled as the motor retainer, which meant a structural part with a job to do was sitting directly against the hot end of the motor casing. The heat softened it, the ejection charge then pushed the weakened plastic out of the way, and the motor slipped out of the back of the airframe. We found it later by walking the field, and nothing else on the vehicle was damaged.

What it led to

That failure is the reason every rocket I have built since uses a metal motor retainer threaded into the aft end, and the reason I treat any part that carries a load and sees motor heat as a structural problem before an aesthetic one. It is also the launch that got me into high power rocketry: by the end of the spring semester I had my Level 1 certification, and by August 2026 my Level 2.

About

I chose aerospace engineering because it's a place that lives on the frontier of human ingenuity. Here, one mistake can mean the difference between a flight that breaks into new territory and a failure. Speaking generally, I enjoy a good challenge. Coupling that with my interest in space and my logical nature, aerospace engineering was the natural path. Specifically, however, I like that “failure” isn't the end of an aerospace engineer's job, as failure can be reconstructed through analysis into a success — and that is something I have firsthand experience with.
